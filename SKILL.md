# ai-raster-scene

Your imagination, rendered: a Python software renderer that turns a small JSON scene (a character-grid map, textures, lights, sprites, GUI overlays, post effects, cameras) into a 320x200, palette-quantised first-person frame, upscaled to a PNG. You can open and look at the PNG yourself, so work like an artist: render, look, fix, re-render.

It is not only for game levels. Any request for a picture that can be told as "a viewer standing somewhere, looking at something" works: a haunted library, a rainy street at night, a shop counter with a menu, a title screen, a character mid-conversation, a moment from a story. The GUI and post layers let the picture *be* a game screenshot, a VHS still, a Game Boy screen or a faded photo.

Scripts live in this skill's `scripts/` folder: `render.py` (engine and CLI), `world.py` (a scene's map read into cells), `assets.py` (every texture, prop, decal and sky by name, and the catalog; drawn in `classic.py` and a kit per world - `biomes.py`, `forest.py`, `vector.py` and the rest, listed in `KITS` - most with the pixel-art toolkit in `pixelart.py`), `skies.py` (sky panoramas), `buildings.py` (building facades in layers), `characters.py` (pixel-art cast), `prefabs.py` (named groups to place with one line), `objects.py` (big things built in 3D out of flat panels), `icons.py` (item icons and held items; the held items are modelled in 3D in `held.py` and rendered to pixel art by `sculpt.py`), `gui.py` + `pixelfont.py` (GUI toolkit), `post.py` (post-processing), `maptools.py` (map authoring). They need `numpy` and `Pillow`; pip install them if an import fails. Read `references/scene_format.md` before writing your first scene in a conversation: every key, the coordinate convention, the pixel-art and draw-op formats, all GUI widgets, effects and presets. `references/assets.md` lists every built-in texture, prop and character, and `references/recipes.md` how to build particular places. `examples/` holds working scenes for every feature; copying the closest one is the fastest start.

## Workflow

1. **Pick the shots.** Decide where the viewer stands, what they look at, the focal point (a character, a lit doorway, a sigil), the light story (moonlight + warm lamps, emergency red, a lone lantern), and the frame's job: plain concept art, a game screenshot (GUI), or a stylised still (post). Two to four shots per location is plenty.
2. **Lay out the map.** Build only what the camera sees plus a margin; maps under ~40x40 render in about a second. Beyond a simple room, write a short Python script with `maptools.Grid` (rect, box, room, line, disc, put); see `examples/mansion_library.py`.
3. **Aim and check the layout first.** Aim each camera with `look_at` (a sprite id, `[x, y]` or `[x, y, z]`) rather than working out an `angle`. Then render `--map`: `<out>_map.png` is a top-down view with walls, lights and their radius, sprites, and each camera's view cone.
4. **Render all shots** with `--sheet` for one contact sheet, and look at it. If the renderer prints `warning: unknown key`, fix that first: a misspelt key is ignored, so part of the scene is silently missing. After each shot it also prints a render report. It flags a frame that is too dark or blown out, a black foreground, a subject that is hidden, cut off, tiny or unlit, a wall in the lens, and a camera inside a wall. Each warning comes with a fix. Act on them unless the darkness or the silhouette is the point.
5. **Critique and fix**, usually two or three passes:
   - Is the foreground readable? A black lower half means no light reaches the cells near the camera: extend a light's radius over them or raise `ambient`.
   - Is the focal point the brightest or most saturated thing in frame? Give characters a light (sprite `"light"`) unless a silhouette is the point.
   - Is anything hiding the subject (props between camera and subject)? Check the map.
   - Too close to a wall (giant texels)? Keep 1.5+ cells from the surface faced; characters read best 1.5-3 units away.
   - Does fog swallow the background? Density 0.03-0.05 outdoors, 0.05-0.08 indoors.
   - Does the GUI cover the subject? Move widgets with `at`; keep speakers in the lower two thirds so bubbles fit above them.
   - Shimmer on floors or ceilings? `"supersample": 2`.
6. **Deliver** the final PNGs themselves, shown to the user however this environment shares files or images, with one line per shot. Offer the scene file if they want to tweak it.

## Commands

```bash
python scripts/render.py scene.json -o out.png              # one image, or out_01.png... for shots
python scripts/render.py scene.json -o out.png --map --sheet # plus layout map and contact sheet
python scripts/render.py scene.json -o out.png --shot 2     # re-render a single shot
python scripts/assets.py catalog.png                        # every texture, prop, character, icon, held item
```

## Characters, ghosts and dialog

- **Cast**: cultist, scientist, guard, nurse, spiritualist, ghost, hybrid, soldier, knight, child, creature, robot, shopkeeper, astronaut, android, alien, dreamer, explorer, hoplite, castaway, diver (in a brass helmet), runner (a cyberpunk with a lit visor), cowboy, witch (her eyes and lantern glow), farmer (with a pitchfork), pirate, samurai (a street samurai: red visor, crimson-lined coat, a sword on the back), hacker (hood, glowing goggles and deck), miner (a gold-rush prospector: slouch hat, grey beard, red flannel, a pick), viking (an iron cap, a red beard, a fur cloak, an axe), monk (shaven, a brown robe and a rope belt), villager (a red kerchief, a green dress and an apron, a basket), and claude (your own avatar, below). People are about 0.63 tall and the child 0.46 - each drawn at twice its grid's resolution and a little taller than it (finer faces, smooth outlines, shaded from the upper left); give the creature a `scale` of 1.2-1.5 when it should loom. For anything else author a 16x32 text-grid sprite in `sprite_defs` (format in the reference; copy the style of `scripts/characters.py`): dark outline colour, 2-3 tones per material, two eye pixels and a mouth, feet on the last rows. Give it `"hd": true` to draw it as the built-ins are. Test it once in a simple lit room. `mirror: true` halves the typing for symmetric figures. Never recreate copyrighted characters; invent originals.
- **Translucency**: `opacity` < 1 for ghosts and glass, `blend: "additive"` for spirits, flames and magic, `aura` for glow, `wobble` for drifting tails. Put translucent sprites against something darker or textured so they read.
- **Answering as yourself.** When someone asks to see you, or asks for an answer "in a picture" or "in character", use your own avatar: the `claude` character, a small figure whose head is a paper lantern lit from inside, in a clay-coloured coat with an open book. `examples/claude_study.py` puts it in its reading room at the top of a tower above the clouds. Shot 2 is a speech bubble for a short answer and shot 4 a dialog panel with its portrait for a longer one: change the text, keep it to one to three lines in your own voice, and render.
- **Dialog**: give the speaker an `id`; `bubble` floats over them, `dialog` is a bottom panel with a portrait (longer lines, off-screen speakers), `caption` for place names. One to three wrapped lines, in the character's voice.

## GUI toolkit (game-screen pictures)

Widgets: `choice` (menu with pointer, hints like costs, disabled options), `title` (big outlined text), `panel` (objectives/notes with checkboxes), `toast` (pickups), `prompt` (`[E] Open`), `bars`, `counter`, `hotbar`, `inventory`, `crosshair`, `compass`, `automap`, `image`, `viewmodel` (held revolver, lantern, flashlight, axe, crowbar, torch). Themes: classic, parchment, terminal, blood, steel. Custom icons and held items are a few draw ops with an automatic outline.

- Compose like a real game screen: HUD hugs the edges (bars top-left, map top-right, hotbar bottom, counter bottom-right), menus sit where they don't cover the subject, and a held item anchors the first-person feel.
- Don't overload: a title card wants only the title; a conversation wants a bubble or dialog plus at most a choice; a full HUD shot can carry 5-7 widgets.
- Pair theme with tone: parchment for occult/fantasy, terminal for labs and computers (with `crt_green`), blood for horror menus, steel for retro-PC parody, classic otherwise.
- A `fade` post effect (0.4-0.5) under an inventory or pause menu sells "the game is paused".

## Post-processing (the final look)

Stack effects or use presets in `"post"`: crt, crt_amber, crt_green, vhs, gameboy, cga, ega, nightvision, cinematic, horror, old_photo, dream, bloodmoon, hurt, and the weather presets storm, downpour, blizzard, autumn, sandstorm, ashfall. Building blocks: grade, bloom, dof, streaks, flare, vignette, grain, rain, snow, splashes, embers, ash, leaves, fireflies, spores, dust, lightning, letterbox, hurt, fade, pixelate, posterize, gradient_map, palette, crt, vhs, chromatic, scanlines.

- Weather goes first, then grading, then lens effects (bloom, vignette), then a palette, then display effects (CRT/VHS). Presets already follow this order. `dof` (depth of field) always runs first, whatever its place.
- **Glow**: `{"fx": "bloom", "source": "light", "strength": 2-2.5, "radius": 2.5, "spread": 0.6}` blooms only what gives off light (neon, lit windows, screens, flames, the sun) and their reflections, so night scenes and neon can glow hard without lifting pale walls; fireflies, embers, spores, a held lantern and a muzzle flash count too (put the bloom after the weather). For a night lit by lamps, windows and flames use `"strength": 1.4, "spread": 0.5`. The plain `bloom` blooms everything bright - right for a dreamy or cinematic soft glow.
- **Depth of field**: add `{"fx": "dof", "blur": 2-3}` to a close shot of a character; it focuses where the shot looks (`look_at`, or `"focus"`: a sprite id or a distance) and softens what is nearer and farther. Keep it for portraits and moments - a wide establishing shot reads better sharp.
- **Streaks and flares**: `{"fx": "streaks"}` puts a little cross of light on the brightest lamps, flames and bulbs, as a star filter does (`"points": 6` makes a star of a low sun; `"points": 2, "length": 50, "strength": 0.4, "color": [0.55, 0.75, 1.3]` the long blue lines of an anamorphic lens through a neon night). `{"fx": "flare"}` strings a lens flare across the frame when the sun or moon is in it - shoot into a low sun with it off to one side. Use them sparingly: one shot in a set, not every shot.
- **Wireframes**: `"wireframes": [{"mesh": "icosahedron", "x": 12, "y": 8, "z": 1, "size": 1.5, "tilt": 20, "color": [0.3, 1, 1]}]` draws a low-poly mesh as lines of light in true 3D - right from every angle, hidden behind what is nearer, mirrored in wet floors; `"solid": true` fills its faces black so it hides what is behind. Built-ins: cube, pyramid, the polyhedra, prism, cylinder, cone, sphere, torus, grid, terrain (mountains), racer, palm, tree, tower, gate, crystal - or give your own `vertices` and `faces`. Not only for Vector World: a hologram over a lab table, a star map, the frame of a building going up.
- One strong look per image. A preset plus one or two tweaks beats a pile of effects.
- `storm` + a `lightning` with `x` placed over the sky makes instant drama; `nightvision` and `vhs` turn any frame into found footage; `old_photo` for "historical record" pictures; `gameboy`/`ega`/`cga` for era jokes and palette studies.

## Making it look good

- **Light is the art direction.** Low ambient (0.05-0.13) and 3-6 coloured point lights. Warm sources against cool ambient reads as 90s horror instantly; one saturated accent (red sigil, teal windows) gives a focal point. Light faces from below with their own candle for menace.
- **Recipes for particular places** are in `references/recipes.md` - read the one you need before writing the scene: woodland, standing stones, jungle, ruins and beaches, inside a temple, a fjord, a glasshouse, a Japanese garden, a sunken temple, a basalt causeway, deserts and canyons, swamps and bayous, under the sea, castles and dungeons, haunted houses, city streets, facades, on a rooftop, a neon bar, a saloon, a mine, a capsule hotel, a clockwork city, industrial and sci-fi, space and sci-fi, on the Moon, alien worlds, an aquarium, dreams and the surreal, sky islands, a mirror salt flat, an aurora ice palace, a glowing jungle, Vector World, Doomed mode, and at landscape scale a kaiju in the city, a kaiju in the country, a volcano island, a mesa desert, a winter valley, a strait bridge, an archipelago and a valley from a giant's height.
- **Exteriors**: open cells show the sky panorama; low walls (hedges 0.7, garden walls 0.9) frame paths without blocking the view. Night exteriors need `ambient` around 0.13 and a few wide cool lights (radius 6-8) spread along the route, or everything past the nearest prop goes black.
- **Sun and moon.** One `{"type": "moon", "azimuth": 300, "elevation": 16}` light lights the whole ground, throws long wall shadows and puts the moon in the sky. A low elevation (10-25) gives the most drama. Aim the camera near its azimuth to frame the disc. Use `sun` in `day`/`dusk`/`overcast` skies. Roofed cells get no sunlight, so interiors still need point lights. A shot's `overrides` can swap moon for sun to show the same place by day.
- **Time of day in one key.** `"time": "dawn"` (or `morning`, `noon`, `afternoon`, `golden`, `sunset`, `dusk`, `night`, or an hour such as 17.5), on the scene or on one shot, sets the sky, puts the sun or moon where the clock has it - east in the morning, west in the evening - and darkens and tints the ambient light and the fog from the scene's own, lighting more of the facades' windows after dark. Mark street lamps and lanterns `"night": true` and they burn only from sunset to sunrise. `{"at": "golden", "azimuth": 100}` turns the sun to where a shot needs it. The quickest way to show one place at several times: the same shot repeated with a different `time`.
- **Give open vistas a horizon.** `"skyline": "mountains"` (or `hills`, `treeline`, `city`) paints distant silhouettes into the sky, so a field, lake or rooftop doesn't end at a bare line.
- **Reflections sell water and rain.** `water`, `ice` and the puddle decals reflect by default; give a wet street `"reflect": 0.3` in its legend entry. Put something bright beyond the reflective floor (lit windows across the ice, crystals across a cave pool, a lamp past a puddle), because a reflection of darkness is invisible.
- **Fences, bars and grilles are see-through walls**: `fence_iron`, `railing`, `picket`, `chainlink`, `bars`, `portcullis`, `lattice`, `window_broken`. The scene shows through the gaps and light passes them. Stack `["portcullis", "castle"]` for a gate, or put `window_broken` in the middle storey of a facade.
- **Steps, daises and altars** are low blocks: a `heights` digit layer (or a small `h`) makes wall cells 0.1-0.9 tall, and a block lower than the eye shows its `top`. Sprites placed on one stand on it, like a throne on a dais or candles on an altar. Ground contact is automatic: every prop and character gets a soft contact shadow.
- **Interiors**: wall height = `ceiling_height`; lights near walls give textures gradients; break long corridors with doorways and spilled light.
- **Composition**: one-point perspective down a corridor or path is the classic; 20-40 degrees off-axis adds variety; a close character on one side with the scene behind makes strong encounter shots.
- **Prefabs place a group with one line**: `{"type": "campsite", "x": 12.5, "y": 20.5}` puts down a fire, logs, stones and its light; `beach_hut` brings its own walls, door, window, roof, torches and lights; `palm_grove`, `jungle_clump`, `fern_bed`, `ruined_columns`, `altar_fire`, `graves`, `street_lamp`, `tiki_torches`, `shrine`, `palm_3d`, `jungle_bush`. `angle` turns one, `seed` varies it. Define your own in `prefab_defs` when a scene repeats a group.
- **Objects are big things built in 3D**: `{"type": "car", "x": 20.4, "y": 45.6, "angle": 270, "paint": "#9a9ea4"}` parks a saloon (`"lights": true` turns its headlamps on); `noodle_stand` is a street-food stand with its awning, curtains, sign, lanterns and bowls on the counter. Unlike a billboard they show the right sides from any angle - so shoot them at an angle - and a figure can stand in front of them or behind. A car is 1.6 long and 0.47 tall. Rock is an object too: a `rock_wall` lines the foot of a wall with overlapping rounded pieces of different heights, their tops cut along a wavy edge, plants on them - better than a flat rock texture wherever a cliff, a cave wall or a tank's rockwork is in view - and a `boulder` object is a solid low-poly rock (the `boulder` prop is a billboard of one); `"rock"` picks `stone`, `reef`, `sandstone`, `snow`, `cave` or `lumen` (glowing moss), and `"lining": "#"` lines every wall face of those map cells with rock. A `column` is a post or a pillar in 3D - wooden piles under a pier, stone pillars in a nave (`"material": "ashlar"` lays it in drums; `broken` snaps it off; `"flutes": 20, "doric": true` in `marble` makes a Greek one) - and a `beam` lies level across them. Timber work: a `trough`, a `shed_roof` (over a porch or a lean-to) and a `gable_roof` (with `walls`, a shed or a barn; `"cover": "thatch"` or `"tile"` and a `chimney` for a cottage); a `market_stand` for a market (its goods heaped in 3D; `paint` its stripes, `goods` its trays), `crate`s (`goods` heaps one, open) and `barrel`s, and a `well` for the green; a `saloon_bar`, `card_table`s (`"game": false` for an empty one), `saloon_chair`s and a `piano` (`lit` burns its candles) for a saloon; rows of `pew`s facing the altar for a chapel; `arcade_cabinet`s (each its own `color`, a game by `seed`) for a bar or an arcade and a `vending_machine` (`paint`) for a street or a corridor; a `sign` of lettering; a `portal` - a door or an arch that opens onto another scene or another part of this one (`to` where it comes out, `scene` which), the view through it lining up as the camera moves, for dreams and magic - and a `screen` that shows another shot (`show`), on a `tv`, a `monitor` or a `wall`, for a picture within the picture: a broadcast, a security camera, a game; `rails` and an `ore_cart` for a mine; a `keel` of rock under a floating island; a `chess_piece` (`piece`, `side`) for a giant game; a `ship_hull` (`heel`, `broken`) for a wreck; a `longship` (`sail`) for the north; a `gable_roof` of `"cover": "glass"` over a glasshouse; a `lighthouse` on a coast; a `stone_lantern` in a garden; `gear`s of brass on the walls of a clockwork city; `heap`s of salt raked up on a salt flat; `standing_stone`s in a circle on a moor (`"casts": true` throws their long shadows at dawn, and shafts of sun between them in a mist). An object backlit by a low sun takes `"fill": 1.0`. A `crystal_cluster` grows crystals whose every facet bends the light through it its own way, like cut stone - put glowing things behind them. A `searchlight` sweeps a beam of light across the sky and a `ray` reaches from one point to another - a laser, a monster's breath; a `cable` sags between two points - power lines, a ropeway; a `bridge` spans a strait (`suspension`) or a river (`stone`); at landscape scale `tower`, `city_block`, `village` and `mountain` build a city, a village and the hills (below).
- **Landscape scale: shrink the world and the camera is a giant.** Call a map cell about 100 m and the eye at 0.5 is fifty metres up: a grid of `city_street_lit` cells, each with a `city_block` object of `tower`s at its middle, makes a city at night round `harbor_water`, and a `kaiju` (0.62 tall) stands in it as a person stands in a room, a `giant_moth` coming for it - `searchlight`s and `ray`s for the beams, `tanks_firing`, `helicopter`s, `blaze`s and `smoke_plume`s; `farmland` crossed by `road_ew`/`road_ns`, `forest_canopy`, a `village`, `mountain`s and a line of `pylon`s with `cable`s make the country; a `mountain` with a `crater` and `lava`, an `ash_plume` and `lava_flow` cells make a volcano; `mountain`s with a `cliff` on `desert_floor` make buttes and mesas; `snow_fields`, `snow_forest`, a `frozen_lake` and a `village` with `"snow": true` and `lit` windows under the `aurora` make winter; `cargo_ship`s, `ferry`s and `sailboat`s go under a `bridge`; `ocean`, `lagoon`, `reef_flat`, `white_sand`, `palm_canopy` and hills of `tropical` rock make an archipelago. Drop the camera to `height` 0.012 for a person's eye looking up at the monster; keep it close (a kaiju a few cells off is a speck) and, over fields, above 0.07 (the ground is drawn to be seen from the air). See Landscape scale in `references/scene_format.md` and the recipes.
- **Words in the world are lettering.** A `sign` object puts any text on a board in 3D (`{"type": "sign", "text": "GENERAL STORE", "style": "painted"}`; styles `painted`, `neon` (glows), `plaque`, `carved`, `chalk`, `gilt`, `poster`), and a `sprite_defs` or `decal_defs` entry with `text` makes a billboard, a card or a decal of it - a shop's name, a neon OPEN in a window, a date over an arch. Invent names; never a real brand.
- **A sequence is a page.** For a comic, a storyboard or "show it in four panels", write a page file and run `python scripts/pages.py page.json -o page.png`: rows of panels, each a shot of any scene (`scene`, `shot`, a `camera` to reframe it) rendered at the panel's own shape, with `caption` boxes, the shots' own speech bubbles, a big `sfx` and a `title`; `"style": "storyboard"` numbers the panels and puts a `note` under each. See `examples/pages/`.
- **Plants have body**: ferns, grass tufts, bromeliads, banana plants, monstera and other low plants stand as crossed planes (`planes` 2 or 3) rather than billboards, so they look solid from any side; trees stay billboards. Set `"planes": 0` for a flat billboard, or `planes` on any sprite to stand it in the world.
- **Props come in variations.** Rocks, grass and dry tufts, ferns, bushes, boulders, mushrooms, logs, stumps, cattails, flowers, poppies, monstera, prickly pear, tumbleweed, kelp, cypress knees, rubble and the trees (spruce, oak, birch, bald cypress, palm, dead tree) are each drawn a few ways (a forest's, a swamp's or a kelp forest's edge stamps a different one for every plant), and every one placed - by hand or by `scatter` - shows one of them, picked by where it stands, so no two stones in a heap match; `"variant": 0` pins one, `"variants": false` on the scene turns it off.
- **Scatter debris rather than placing it.** `scatter` fills a floor with weighted, size-varied clutter from one line (`{"of": "stones", "on": "s", "density": 1.5}`); sets are `stones`, `snow`, `rubble`, `forest`, `undergrowth`, `autumn`, `desert`, `swamp`, `seabed`, `shore`, `litter`, `wreckage`, `cave`, `alien`, `derelict`, `jungle`, `beach`, `ruins`. Density 0.3 is a dusting, 1.5+ is a stony beach; `scale` multiplies every item's size (0.09 for trees seen from a giant's height). It avoids cameras and speaking characters by itself.
- **Reach for `grime` when somewhere is neglected.** One 0-1 number raises surface variation, debris density and wall/floor wear together, which is usually what "this place is filthy" means.
- **Dress the floor.** An empty floor is the clearest sign of an unfinished frame. Scatter a few ground props (`rocks`, `rubble`, `bones`, `grass_tuft`, `reeds`, `sack`, `bucket`, `broken_barrel`) and stamp `decals` (puddles, stains, cracks, scorch, moss) along the route the camera actually looks down. A handful placed in shot beats a carpet placed everywhere - check with `--map` that they fall inside the view cone.
- **Decals over new textures.** Wet patches, blood, vines, soot, water damage and cracks are `decals`, not new wall textures: they cost nothing, stack, and can sit anywhere on a surface. Use `puddle_shine` wherever a light reaches and `puddle_dark` only in dim interiors - a dark puddle on a lit floor reads as a hole.
- **Light the fixture, not the air.** Pair every light with a visible source: `sconce` and `brazier` on walls and floors, `ceiling_lamp` and `chandelier` hung at `z` just under `ceiling_height`, `lamp_post` outdoors.
- **Fog with a shape.** Add `ground`, `height` and `noise` to `fog` for mist that lies low and drifts in banks, so stones, graves and trees rise out of it. `glow` gives every lamp a halo. With a `sun` or `moon` light, `shafts` turns the light into beams through doorways, broken windows, a fallen roof, standing stones and trees. A low sun (5-15 degrees) behind the subject gives the strongest beams. Keep `shafts` around 1 when the camera looks into the sun, and give the fog a `color` lighter than the dark around it. See `examples/misty_moor.py` and `cathedral_ruin.py`. Coloured glass colours the sun that comes through it: put `lancet_window`, `rose_window` or `stained_glass` in a storey of a wall, stand the sun in front of it (within about 20 degrees of straight on), and its pattern lies on the floor and its colours hang in the beams - `examples/glass_chapel.py`.
- **Weather is in the world.** Rain, snow, leaves, ash and dust fall behind walls and never under a roof, shrinking and fading into the fog with distance. For a downpour use the `downpour` preset with `"wet": 0.6-0.8` (wet ground mirrors the lamps and windows) and a little fog `glow`. `blizzard`, `autumn`, `sandstorm` and `ashfall` are one word each. Use `fireflies`, `spores` and `embers` for magic, alien air and fire. See `examples/rain_alley.py`.
- **Shots from behind.** Give a character `facing` (the compass direction it looks) and put the camera behind it: you get its back. That's the shot of someone looking out over the view, walking away down a wet street, or standing in a beam of light. Every built-in character has a back view.
- **Water sits low.** Give water cells `"drop": 0.12-0.2` so the surface reads below its bank instead of painted onto the floor; sprites in those cells rest on the water.
- **Surface variation is on by default.** `surface_noise` (0.10) mottles wall, floor and ceiling albedo in world space so textures stop reading as tiles. Each material scales it by its own appetite (plaster 0.7, cobble 3.0), so one number already suits a mixed scene. Raise it toward 0.16 for a grimy derelict place, drop it to ~0.05 for somewhere clean and new, set `0` for flat poster-like frames, and use `{"amount": 0.1, "by_texture": {"cobble": 0.3}}` to pin one material.
- **Custom materials**: `{"base": name, "tint": [r,g,b]}` or a PNG path in any texture slot; new procedural textures, props or decals go in a kit module (see `KITS` in `assets.py`).

## Limits to be honest about

A 2.5D raycaster: one ground floor (plus shallow `drop` cells for water and low blocks for steps and daises you look down onto), one ceiling height, grid-aligned block walls, camera-facing sprites (characters have a front and a back but no side view), no slopes, walkable upper floors or animation. Reflections are screen-space, on floors only. When a request needs something it cannot show (a cliffside staircase, a bridge over a gap, a balcony to stand on, a round tower, a character seen side-on, a wide aerial view), approximate it and say so in a line, or suggest the closest shot that works. If the request is better served by a non-retro image, say that this tool makes 90s-game-style pictures.
